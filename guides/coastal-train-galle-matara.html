The Galle-Matara Coastal Train Journey

South Coast, Sri Lanka • 6 min read

Sri Lanka's coastal railway line, running along the southwest coast between Colombo and Matara, produces one of the most genuinely scenic stretches of train travel anywhere in Asia — and the section between Galle and Matara, which passes directly through Ahangama, Weligama, and Mirissa, is the best part of it. For long stretches the track runs close enough to the water that waves are visible right from the open doorway. It's also absurdly cheap compared to a tuk-tuk or private car for the same distance.

Why it's worth doing on purpose

Plenty of visitors end up on this line by accident, using it as a cheap way to get from one beach town to the next — which is reason enough. But the Galle-Matara stretch is worth treating as an experience in its own right, not just transport. Third class carriages have open doorways you're allowed to sit in (carefully, holding on), and on a clear afternoon the light over the ocean during the ride is one of the more memorable low-key experiences on this coast.

The practical route

Local trains run this line multiple times a day in both directions, stopping at (among others) Galle, Unawatuna-adjacent Boossa, Ahangama, Weligama, and Mirissa, before terminating in Matara. Journey time for the full Galle-Matara stretch is roughly an hour, though most visitors use it for a shorter hop — Ahangama to Galle, or Weligama to Mirissa, for example. Tickets are bought at the station just before departure and are inexpensive enough that cost is never really a factor in the decision.

Second or third class, not first. This line generally doesn't run the reserved, air-conditioned first-class carriages found on the more touristy Kandy routes. Second and third class are unreserved, ordinary seating — which is exactly what you want here, since the open doors and windows are the whole point. Don't expect (or pay for) a "scenic train experience" package; just turn up and buy a ticket.

What to expect on board

Trains can be genuinely crowded at peak times, particularly on weekends and around school and work commute hours, so don't assume you'll get a seat, let alone a doorway spot. Off-peak, particularly midday, is far more comfortable and still gives excellent light for photos. Vendors sometimes walk the carriages selling snacks and drinks, which is part of the charm rather than an inconvenience.

Safety notes

Sitting in an open doorway is a well-known and broadly tolerated part of local train culture on this line, but it isn't without risk — hold on properly, keep limbs inside when passing other trains or platforms, and never lean out further than you can comfortably recover from. It's not a ride to attempt if you're travelling with young children who can't be trusted to stay seated, or if the train is crowded enough that door space is genuinely unsafe to access.

Combining it with a day out

The coastal train pairs naturally with a Galle Fort day trip from Ahangama — ride the train one way and a tuk-tuk or car back, or vice versa, so you get the scenic leg without doubling up on the same journey. See our Galle Fort guide and day trip guide for how to build a full day around it. It's also a genuinely pleasant way to reach Mirissa for a whale watching morning instead of a pre-dawn tuk-tuk ride.

Practical tips

  • Buy your ticket at the station window just before your train — there's no need to book in advance for second or third class on this line.
  • Travel light. Carriages can be tight, and there's limited luggage space in unreserved seating.
  • Midday and early afternoon tend to be less crowded than morning and evening commuter windows.
  • Keep valuables secure in your bag rather than loose pockets, as with any crowded public transport.

Of all the ways to get between the south coast beach towns, the coastal train is the one visitors most often say they wish they'd done more of — cheap, scenic, and a genuine slice of everyday Sri Lankan life rather than a tourist-only experience.
